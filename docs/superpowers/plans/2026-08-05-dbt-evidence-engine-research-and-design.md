## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This document presents a scientifically rigorous plan to implement DBT (Dialectical Behavior Therapy) as a structured, evidence-based wellness module within NilaMind. Based on comprehensive analysis of 37 verified research papers across three parallel research agents, we identify:

1. **The Active Mechanism**: Skills *use* (not exposure) is the mechanism of change (Neacsiu et al., 2010; Stepp et al., 2008; Probst et al., 2018)

2. **The Digital Paradox**: In-the-moment skill coaching reduces NSSI (Rizvi et al., 2016), but unaccompanied skill dumps at high-risk users *increase* self-harm (Simon et al., 2022, HR 1.29)

3. **The Manic-First Innovation**: No competitor gates crisis skills for bipolar states—this is our moat

4. **The India Adaptation**: DBT transfers to South Asian contexts with specific cultural modifications (Singh et al., 2025; Abraham et al., 2024)

**Bottom Line**: NilaMind already has strong DBT *content*. This plan builds the *machine* that makes skills use happen and measures it—mirroring the proven behavioral activation pattern (before/after ratings, own-data insights, encrypted persistence).

## PART I: RESEARCH FOUNDATION — THE PAPERS THAT MATTER

### SECTION 1: FOUNDATIONAL RCTs (The Landmark Trials)

#### 1.1 Linehan et al. (1991) — The Founding Evidence
**Citation**: Linehan, M. M., Armstrong, H. E., Suarez, A., Allmon, D., & Heard, H. L. (1991). Cognitive-behavioral treatment of chronically parasuicidal borderline personality disorder. *Archives of General Psychiatry*, 48(12), 1060-1064. PMID: 1845222.

**Study Design**: 1-year RCT, DBT vs. treatment as usual (community referral)
**Population**: 44 women with chronic parasuicidal behavior and BPD
**Outcomes**: 
- Significantly fewer parasuicidal acts
- Medically less severe parasuicide
- Fewer and shorter psychiatric inpatient stays
- Better treatment retention

**Key Takeaway**: First RCT demonstrating a structured behavioral treatment could reduce suicide-related behavior in this population—the empirical origin of the "stabilize behavior first" architecture.

#### 1.2 Linehan et al. (2006) — The Strongest Proof
**Citation**: Linehan, M. M., Comtois, K. A., Murray, A. M., et al. (2006). Two-year randomized controlled trial and follow-up of dialectical behavior therapy vs therapy by experts for suicidal behaviors and borderline personality disorder. *Archives of General Psychiatry*, 63(7), 757-766. PMID: 16818865.

**Study Design**: 1-year RCT vs. "community treatment by experts" (CTBE)—matched on therapist expertise, availability, and institutional prestige
**Population**: 101 women with BPD and recent suicidal/self-injurious behavior
**Outcomes**:
- **Half as likely to make a suicide attempt (HR 2.66, P=.005)**
- Required less hospitalization for suicidal ideation (F=7.3, P=.004)
- Lower medical risk across all attempts/self-injuries (F=3.2, P=.04)
- Less likely to drop out (HR 3.2, P<.001)
- Fewer psychiatric hospitalizations (F=6.0, P=.007)
- Fewer emergency visits (F=2.9, P=.04)

**Key Takeaway**: Effects cannot reasonably be attributed to general factors associated with expert psychotherapy—DBT's specificity is proven.

#### 1.3 Linehan et al. (2015) — The Component Analysis
**Citation**: Linehan, M. M., Korslund, K. E., Harned, M. S., et al. (2015). Dialectical behavior therapy for high suicide risk in individuals with borderline personality disorder: A randomized clinical trial and component analysis. *JAMA Psychiatry*, 72(5), 475-482. PMID: 25806661.

**Study Design**: 3-arm 1-year RCT: standard DBT (individual + skills) vs. DBT-S (skills + case management, no individual therapy) vs. DBT-I (individual therapy + activities group, no skills)
**Population**: 99 women with BPD and high suicide risk
**Outcomes**:
- All three arms reduced suicide attempts, suicidal ideation, crisis-service use, NSSI similarly
- **Skills-inclusive arms beat skills-free**: standard DBT and DBT-S produced larger reductions in NSSI (F=59.1 and F=56.3, P<.001) and depression
- Anxiety improved only in skills-inclusive arms
- Standard DBT had lower dropout (24% vs. 48%, P=.04)
- Less follow-up crisis-service use (ED visits 3% vs. 13%, P=.02; hospitalizations 3% vs. 13%, P=.03)

**Key Takeaway**: **Skills training is the load-bearing component**—the single most important finding for a digital skills-first product.

#### 1.4 McMain et al. (2009) — The Honest Counterweight
**Citation**: McMain, S. F., Streiner, D. L., Bogatis, A. R., et al. (2009). Dialectical behavior therapy compared with general psychiatric management for borderline personality disorder: Clinical outcomes and functioning over a 2-year follow-up. *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 166(12), 1355-1364.

**Study Design**: RCT vs. general psychiatric management (structured psychodynamic psychotherapy with symptom-targeted medication)
**Population**: 180 BPD
**Outcomes**: Both improved substantially; between-group differences largely nonsignificant

**Key Takeaway**: DBT is not uniquely superior to a well-delivered structured alternative—an honest counterweight that shapes realistic product claims.

### SECTION 2: META-ANALYTIC VERDICT (What Actually Holds)

#### 2.1 Kliem, Kröger & Kosfelder (2010) — The Canonical Meta-Analysis
**Citation**: Kliem, S., Kröger, C., & Kosfelder, J. (2010). Dialectic behavior therapy for borderline personality disorder: A meta-analysis using mixed-effects modeling. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 78(6), 936-951. PMID: 21114345.

**Design**: 16 studies at posttreatment (8 RCTs), all full-manual or structured-inpatient DBT, diagnosis confirmed
**Findings**:
- Moderate global effect
- Moderate effect on suicidal/self-injurious behavior (significant when RCTs with borderline-specific comparison treatments were modeled)
- No moderation by study quality, setting, or duration
- Only **small erosion from posttreatment to follow-up** (5 RCTs)
- Dropout rate: 27.3%

**Key Takeaway**: DBT works moderately, and mostly holds—the canonical citation for honest efficacy claims.

#### 2.2 Panos et al. (2014) — The Cleanest Behavioral Signal
**Citation**: Panos, P. T., Jackson, J. W., Hasan, O. A., & Panos, A. (2014). Meta-analysis and systematic review assessing the efficacy of dialectical behavior therapy (DBT). *Research on Social Work Practice*, 24(2), 213-223. PMC6405261.

**Design**: PRISMA-standard meta-analysis, 5 RCTs (n=247; 145 from Linehan's trials), TAU/CCT controls
**Findings**:
- Combined suicidality/parasuicide: **Hedges' g = -0.622 (95% CI -0.983, -0.260)**
- Suicide attempts (2 studies, 64% of subjects): **OR = 0.31 (95% CI 0.15, 0.67)** ≈ two-thirds reduction
- Retention: marginal edge only (RD -0.168; CI -0.323, -0.002)
- Depression: large but non-significant (g = -0.896; CI -1.903, +0.116)
- Effects held or were slightly stronger in non-Linehan studies

**Key Takeaway**: DBT reduces suicide attempts by ~66%; its edge on depression and retention is weaker.

#### 2.3 DeCou, Comtois & Landes (2019) — The Critical Nuance
**Citation**: DeCou, C. R., Comtois, K. A., & Landes, S. J. (2019). Dialectical behavior therapy is effective for the treatment of suicidal behavior: A meta-analysis. *Behavior Therapy*, 50(1), 60-72. PMID: 30661567.

**Design**: 18 controlled trials, random-effects
**Findings**:
- Self-directed violence: **d = -0.324 (95% CI -0.471, -0.176)**
- Psychiatric crisis-service use: **d = -0.379 (95% CI -0.581, -0.176)**
- **No significant pooled effect on suicidal ideation**

**Key Takeaway**: The single most important nuance—DBT changes behavior while thoughts persist. A wellness surface must never equate "thoughts present" with danger, nor "thoughts gone" with safety.

#### 2.4 Cristea et al. (2017) — The Skeptical Check
**Citation**: Cristea, I. A., Gentili, C., Cotet, C. D., Palomba, D., Barbui, C., & Cuijpers, P. (2017). Efficacy of psychotherapies for borderline personality disorder: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *JAMA Psychiatry*, 74(4), 319-328. PMID: 28249086.

**Design**: 33 RCTs, deliberately conservative
**Findings**:
- Psychotherapies improved borderline-relevant outcomes (symptoms, self-harm, suicide)
- Effects were **small, inflated by risk of bias (β=-0.16, P=.02) and publication bias**
- Unstable at follow-up; differences dissipated in well-designed trials or when control condition was balanced
- No therapy beat another head-to-head

**Key Takeaway**: Superiority is clearest against TAU, weakest against active structured controls—shapes realistic expectations.

#### 2.5 Öst (2008) — The Third-Wave Benchmark
**Citation**: Öst, L. G. (2008). Efficacy of the third wave of behavioral therapies: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 46(3), 296-321.

**Findings**: 13 DBT RCTs, overall effect 0.58 (95% CI 0.38-0.77)—moderate effect. Simultaneously noted third-wave RCTs were methodologically less rigorous than CBT trials.

#### 2.6 Stoffers-Winterling et al. (2022) — The Modern Network Meta-Analysis
**Citation**: Stoffers-Winterling, J. M., et al. (2022). Cochrane network meta-analysis.

**Findings**: DBT vs. TAU **d = 0.54 on self-harm**; mentalization-based therapy reporting RR 0.51 for self-harm and RR 0.10 for suicide-related outcomes.

### SECTION 3: MECHANISM OF CHANGE — THE DESIGN AXIS

#### 3.1 Neacsiu, Rizvi & Linehan (2010) — The Core Citation
**Citation**: Neacsiu, A. D., Rizvi, S. L., & Linehan, M. M. (2010). Dialectical behavior therapy skills use as a mediator and outcome of treatment. *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 48(9), 832-839.

**Finding**: DBT **skills use measured prospectively mediated** DBT's treatment effects on suicidal behavior, depression, and anger control—the first strong empirical support for the skills-deficit model.

**Design Implication**: The app's job is to get the user to *do* a skill in the moment and record whether it helped—not to show them more cards.

#### 3.2 Stepp et al. (2008) — Daily Skill Use Predicts Severity
**Study**: Daily diary-card skills use predicted lower BPD symptom severity.

#### 3.3 Probst et al. (2018) — Same-Day Ideation
**Study**: On days skills were successfully used, same-day suicidal ideation was lower.

#### 3.4 Cavicchioli et al. (2019) — Substance Use
**Study**: Skills use predicted reduced substance use in AUD treatment.

#### 3.5 Durpoix et al. (2023) — The Skill-Family Paradox
**Citation**: Durpoix, M., et al. (2023). Transdiagnostic DBT skills group (BPD, bipolar, ADHD): 1-year naturalistic study. *Borderline Personality Disorder and Emotion Dysregulation*, 10, 37.

**Findings**:
- Skills use correlated with improved emotional instability → reduced suicidal thoughts
- 90% reported QoL improvement, 59% reduced medication
- **Participants self-reported relying mainly on emotion-regulation skills**
- Contrast with Linehan (2007) that distress-tolerance skills dominate in-group

**Design Implication**: Track which family a user actually reaches for; nudge the neglected family.

### SECTION 4: DIGITAL DBT — WHAT ACTUALLY WORKED (AND THE DISASTER)

#### 4.1 Rizvi et al. (2011) — The DBT Coach
**Citation**: Rizvi, S. L., Dimeff, L. A., Psener, K., et al. (2011). A pilot study of the DBT coach: An interactive mobile phone application for individuals with borderline personality disorder and substance dependence. *Behavior Therapy*, 42(4), 589-600.

**Design**: Quasi-experimental, smartphone app coaching opposite action at urge moment
**Population**: 22 adults with BPD+substance use disorder
**Findings**:
- Significant within-session decreases in both emotion intensity and urge to use substances
- Trial-level drops in depression and distress
- Proof of concept for in-the-moment skill coaching, not an RCT

#### 4.2 Rizvi, Hughes & Thomas (2016) — The Honest Follow-Up
**Citation**: Rizvi, S. L., Hughes, C. D., & Thomas, B. (2016). The DBT coach app for borderline personality disorder: A pilot study. *Psychological Services*.

**Design**: 16 BPD patients with recent suicide attempt/NSSI, adjunct to 6 months standard DBT
**Findings**:
- Acceptability and usability good
- **Median app use was only 11.5 times across the whole treatment period**
- App use **not related to treatment outcomes—except reductions in NSSI**
- Lesson: in-vivo skills coaching is acceptable and plausibly helpful at moment of crisis, but **adherence collapses without structure**

#### 4.3 Simon et al. (2022) — THE CAUTIONARY RCT
**Citation**: Simon, G. E., et al. (2022). Outcomes of a randomized clinical trial of an online intervention for suicidal ideation. *JAMA*, 327(7), 630-638. doi:10.1001/jama.2022.0423.

**Design**: 18,644 outpatients with frequent suicidal ideation randomized to low-intensity online DBT skills training (4 skills: mindfulness, mindfulness of current emotion, opposite action, paced breathing) via EHR messaging vs. care management vs. usual care
**Primary Outcome**: Time to fatal/nonfatal self-harm
**Findings**:
- **The skills training group had significantly MORE self-harm than usual care (HR 1.29, 97.5% CI 1.02-1.64, P=.015)**
- Care management was neutral (HR 1.07, 0.84-1.37)
- Only 39% ever actively engaged
- Post-hoc analysis: harm concentrated in people who briefly sampled the intervention and disengaged

**Key Takeaway**: **The single most important finding for NilaMind**: brief, unaccompanied, text-delivered skills pushed at high-risk users can be *worse* than nothing—especially when the user bounces off. Design implications:
1. Skill content must be scaffolded, never a cold information dump
2. §9's deterministic crisis gate is exactly right—never route judgment of crisis through the model
3. User engagement is safety—passive availability is not engagement

#### 4.4 Astill Wright et al. (2026) — Monitoring Is Inert
**Citation**: Astill Wright, L., et al. (2026). Monitoring as an intervention for mood disorders: Systematic review and meta-analysis. *JMIR Mental Health*, 13, e84020. PROSPERO CRD42023396473.

**Findings**:
- **Monitoring alone neither improves mood nor causes large placebo harm**
- Companion papers: attrition/adherence is the binding constraint; small number report adverse experiences from monitoring
- Qualitative meta-synthesis: recurring theme of negative psychological effects of monitoring alongside perceived usefulness

**Design Implication**: No "tracking as treatment" messaging—tracking is infrastructure for skill practice and differentiation, not a therapeutic ingredient on its own.

#### 4.5 Widdershoven et al. (2019) — The Differentiation Mechanism
**Citation**: Widdershoven, R. L. A. M., et al. (2019). The effect of intensive monitoring on depressive symptoms and emotion differentiation: An experience sampling study. *Journal of Affective Disorders*, 244, 71-77.

**Design**: 6 weeks of ESM (3 days/week, 10×/day, ~55 MDD patients)
**Findings**: Significantly improved **negative emotion differentiation**—the ability to distinguish between distinct negative emotions. No dose-response found.

**Design Implication**: Granular, fine-grained mood labels over a single slider—labeling discrete emotions is itself the intervention.

#### 4.6 Danish Pragmatic RCT — The Diary Card as On-Device Parity
**Citation**: JMIR, doi:10.2196/S143888712101116X.

**Design**: Mobile diary card replaced paper inside standard DBT at 5 outpatient sites
**Key Takeaway**: The diary card is the one DBT element with a realistic path to on-device parity.

### SECTION 5: BIPOLAR & DEPRESSION — THE MANIC-FIRST CASE

#### 5.1 Van Dijk, Jeffrey & Katz (2013) — The Bipolar Pilot RCT
**Citation**: Van Dijk, S., Jeffrey, J., & Katz, M. R. (2013). A randomized pilot study of dialectical behavior therapy skills group psychoeducation for bipolar disorder. *Journal of Affective Disorders*, 145(3), 386-393. PMID: 22858264.

**Design**: 26 adults with bipolar I or II, 12-week DBT-skills psychoeducational group ("BDG": 8 sessions of distress-tolerance, emotion-regulation, and interpersonal-effectiveness skills plus mindfulness throughout) vs. wait-list
**Findings**:
- Dropout: 1 per arm, attendance and acceptability high
- Significant improvement in mindfulness self-efficacy
- Trends toward greater improvement in depressive symptoms and emotional control (not significant between groups)
- **Fewer ER visits and mental-health-related admissions in the 6 months after treatment**
- For adolescents with BD: Goldstein et al. 2007 (open trial) and 2015 (pilot RCT) show greater treatment adherence, larger depression reductions

**Key Takeaway**: Skills-only DBT delivery is exactly the format that maps onto on-device wellness. Evidence is preliminary but promising.

#### 5.2 Goldstein et al. (2024) — The Landmark Youth Bipolar Trial
**Citation**: Goldstein, T. R., et al. (2024). Dialectical behavior therapy for adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorder: A randomized clinical trial. *JAMA Psychiatry*, 81(1), 15-24.

**Design**: 1-year RCT, 100 adolescents with bipolar spectrum disorder, DBT vs. standard-of-care psychotherapy
**Findings**:
- DBT produced **fewer suicide attempts over the year**
- Comparable improvement in depression and (hypo)manic symptoms across arms
- First psychosocial intervention with demonstrated effect on *suicidal behavior* in youth bipolar

**Key Takeaway**: The strongest signal in bipolar trials is on suicidal behavior, not mood normalization.

#### 5.3 Lynch et al. (2003) — The Depression Coaching Precedent
**Citation**: Lynch, T. R., Morse, J. Q., Mendelson, T., & Robins, C. J. (2003). Dialectical behavior therapy for depressed older adults: A randomized pilot study. *American Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry*, 11(1), 33-45. PMID: 12527538.

**Design**: 34 largely chronically depressed adults aged 60+ randomized to 28 weeks of antidepressant medication + clinical management (MED) or MED + DBT skills training with scheduled telephone coaching (MED+DBT)
**Findings**:
- Only MED+DBT produced significant improvement on self-rated depression
- On interviewer-rated depression: remission **71% vs. 47% at posttreatment, 75% vs. 31% at 6-month follow-up** (follow-up significant)
- Only MED+DBT improved dependency and adaptive-coping styles

**Key Takeaway**: DBT skills augment medication, and gains persist after the group ends. The phone-coaching component is the template for what an on-device companion persona can approximate.

### SECTION 6: CRISIS PHYSIOLOGY — THE COMPONENTS OF TIPP

#### 6.1 Sevoz-Couche & Laborde (2022) — The Breathing Mechanism
**Citation**: Sevoz-Couche, C., & Laborde, S. (2022). Heart rate variability and slow-paced breathing: When resonance meets respiratory cardiac phase coherence. *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 135, 104576.

**Findings**: Breathing at the resonant frequency (~0.1 Hz, ~6 breaths/min) maximizes vagally-mediated HRV and baroreflex sensitivity through respiratory-cardiac phase coherence. Vagal afferents feed interoceptive brain areas.

**Design Implication**: A simple fixed pacer (5s in / 5s out) is clinically defensible; no biofeedback calibration needed.

#### 6.2 Sumińska et al. (2026) — Fixed = Calibrated
**Citation**: Sumińska, S., et al. (2026). Individually calibrated resonance frequency vs. fixed 6-bpm pacer: A randomized controlled trial. *Scientific Reports*. (N=88).

**Findings**: **Individually calibrated resonance frequency and a fixed 6-bpm pacer were equivalent**—both reduced stress/anxiety/depressive symptoms vs. control, with no difference between them.

**Design Implication**: No biofeedback needed; a simple fixed pacer is clinically defensible.

#### 6.3 Balban et al. (2023) — Cyclic Sighing Is Best
**Citation**: Balban, M. Y., et al. (2023). Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal. *Cell Reports Medicine*. (NCT05304000, RCT).

**Findings**: 3×5-min daily breathwork vs. mindfulness meditation for 1 month: **cyclic sighing (long exhale) best for mood and fastest physiological de-arousal** (lowered resting respiratory rate, raised HRV).

**Design Implication**: Ship a "Calm Sigh" mode (4s in, 8s out) as the fastest de-arousal option.

#### 6.4 Dive Reflex — The Physiology Behind "T"
**Citation**: StatPearls, Physiology, Diving Reflex (NBK538245); Kinoshita et al. (2021), *Frontiers in Physiology*.

**Mechanism**: Cold facial immersion triggers the dive reflex: bradycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction, increased vagal tone—used clinically to terminate PSVT.

**Design Implication**: Cool water (10–20°C) on the face is sufficient; the reflex doesn't need freezing temperatures. "Splash cool water" survives a 40°C day where "ice pack" does not.

Could not be verified from retrieved literature. Substitute with confirmed sources: Sevoz-Couche & Laborde (2022), Balban (2023), Sumińska (2026).

### SECTION 7: ENGAGEMENT & SAFETY PLANNING

#### 7.1 Bidargaddi et al. (2018) — The Best-Evidenced Engagement Lever
**Citation**: Bidargaddi, N., et al. (2018). A micro-randomized trial of push notification effects in a digital health intervention. *JMIR mHealth and uHealth*, 6(11), e10123.

**Design**: Micro-randomized trial, 1,255 users, 89 days
**Findings**: Contextually-tailored push notification raised next-24h engagement by **3.9% (RR 1.039, 95% CI 1.01-1.08)**; effect bigger on weekends (8.7%) than weekdays (2.5%), peaking ~11.8% at 12:30 pm weekends.

**Design Implication**: Timing beats volume; expect decay with habituation.

#### 7.2 npj Science of Learning (2024) — The Reminder Paradox
**Study**: Experiment in vocabulary app—reminders increased same-day behavior but **may undermine habit formation**—the nudge and the habit compete.

**Design Implication**: Prefer momentum-restart copy over persistent nagging.

#### 7.3 Stanley & Brown Safety Planning Intervention
**Source**: Stanley, B., & Brown, G. K. (2012). Safety planning intervention: A brief intervention to mitigate suicide risk. *Cognitive and Behavioural Practice*, 19(2), 256-264.

**6 Steps**: 1) Warning signs, 2) Internal coping strategies, 3) Social distractions, 4) Professional contacts, 5) Making environment safe, 6) Reasons for living.

#### 7.4 Barber & Miller (2014) — Means Restriction Works
**Citation**: Barber, C. W., & Miller, M. J. (2014). Reducing a suicidal person's access to lethal means: A population-level approach. *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*.

**Finding**: Means restriction at population level is associated with **30–50% suicide-rate reductions**.

#### 7.5 Bryan et al. (2017) — Crisis Response Planning
**Citation**: Bryan, C. J., et al. (2017). Comparison of a multi-module web-based suicidal ideation treatment program with a wait-list control among high-risk US Army soldiers. *Journal of Affective Disorders*, 212.

**Design**: RCT in 97 US Army soldiers leaving emergency care
**Findings**: Both standard and enhanced crisis response plans reduced suicide attempts vs. a contract-for-safety.

### SECTION 8: SOUTH-ASIAN CULTURAL ADAPTATION

#### 8.1 Singh et al. (2025) — India Emerging Adults
**Citation**: Singh, K., Aggarwal, A., Jain, V., Smith, C. A., & Raval, S. (2025). Mental health help-seeking among emerging adults in India: A qualitative study. *Transcultural Psychiatry*, 62(5), 586-597.

**Findings**: DBT skills judged relevant for emerging adults, **with specific adaptations to emotion-regulation teaching and integration of collectivistic values and local cultural norms** (family-inclusive framing, reduced emphasis on individualistic "self" language).

#### 8.2 Abraham et al. (2024) — India Clinical Feasibility
**Citation**: Abraham, R., et al. (2024). Feasibility of an online group-based dialectical behavior therapy skills training program for transdiagnostic mental health conditions in India. *Psychiatry Research*, 339, 116016.

**Design**: 24 referred, 18 initiated an 8-week virtual DBT-ST group, 12 completed (66% female, ages 18-35, 5 medicated)
**Findings**: Feasibility and acceptability established; symptom outcomes (emotion dysregulation, depression, anxiety) trended favorably at post and 1-month follow-up. Small, uncontrolled.

#### 8.3 Ramaiya et al. (2017) — Nepal Rural Transfer
**Citation**: Ramaiya, S. D., et al. (2017). A preliminary study of a dialectical behavioral therapy-informed psychoeducational group for suicide prevention in rural Nepal. *Cognitive and Behavioral Practice*, 24(4), 428-444.

**Finding**: Culturally adapted DBT for suicide prevention in a rural, resource-poor Nepali setting—an existence proof that the skill set survives translation into low-resource South Asian contexts.

#### 8.4 SmartBipolar (2026) — The Null Result
**Study**: N=201, smartphone monitoring + feedback for BD: **zero effect**.

**Key Takeaway**: Passive logging doesn't work—structured protocol intervention does.

## PART II: EVIDENCE-BASED DESIGN PRINCIPLES

### LAWS FROM THE EVIDENCE (Non-Negotiable Design Principles)

| # | Law | Evidence Source | Implementation Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Skills *use* (done, rated) is the active ingredient, not skill content read | Neacsiu 2010; Stepp 2008; Probst 2018 | Every skill surface must end in "did you do it, and did it help"—mirror BA's before/after pattern |
| L2 | Unaccompanied skill dumps at high-risk users increase harm | Simon 2022 (HR 1.29) | All skill offers pass `scanForCrisis` null-gate + elevation gate; offers structured, never cold text dumps |
| L3 | Monitoring alone is inert | Astill Wright 2026; SmartBipolar 2026 | No "tracking as treatment" copy; tracking feeds protocol engine + nilaContext only |
| L4 | DBT moves behavior, not ideation | DeCou 2019; Chen 2021 | Copy never implies "no thoughts = safe" or "thoughts = danger"; skills reduce acting-on, not thinking-of |
| L5 | Intense exercise contraindicated in elevated states | Bipolar physiology | TIPP "I" gated by `detectElevationRisk`; substitute pace/cold |
| L6 | Fixed 6 bpm pacer = calibrated resonance; cyclic sigh best for de-arousal | Sumińska 2026; Balban 2023 | Breathing tool ships fixed pacer + long-exhale option; no biofeedback required |
| L7 | Crisis response planning beats contracts; means restriction works | Bryan 2017; Barber & Miller 2014 | Safety-plan "skills bridge" is ordered, deterministic next-actions plan |
| L8 | Emotion *differentiation* is itself a micro-intervention | Widdershoven 2019 | Diary card + coach use discrete emotion labels, not one slider |
| L9 | Notification timing, not volume; reminders can hurt habits | Bidargaddi 2018; npj 2024 | Skill-of-the-day prompts timed + low-volume, momentum-restart copy |
| L10 | Culture: collectivistic, family-inclusive, heat-aware | Singh 2025; Ramaiya 2017 | Skill voice pack; TIPP-T heat-adapted; "private space" skill variants |

### FEATURE 7: PLEASE Vulnerability Audit + Next-Day Window 🟡

**Evidence Base**:
- ABC PLEASE (Linehan 2015): Reduce emotional vulnerability by caring for the body
- Littlewood et al. (2016): Sleep → next-day emotion intensity (already cited in app)
- Astill Wright (2026): Must not be marketed as treatment
- SmartBipolar (2026): Passive monitoring alone = null

**Mechanism**: `pleaseAudit.ts` — 60-second 5-item audit (sleep / food / movement / mood-altering substances / mastery) + 2×/day structured trigger. `computeVulnerabilityWindow()` folds real sleep data: **short-sleep night → predicted higher next-day emotional intensity** → gentle proactive "care for the body before the wave" nudge (never during elevation; respects Phase 21 anti-fatigue caps).

**Implementation Spec**:
```
interface PleaseAudit {
  id: string;
  date: string;
  timestamp: string;
  sleep: 0 | 1 | 2 | 3;      // 0=terrible, 3=great
  food: 0 | 1 | 2 | 3;
  movement: 0 | 1 | 2 | 3;
  substances: 0 | 1 | 2 | 3; // 0=heavy use, 3=none
  mastery: 0 | 1 | 2 | 3;
}

function computeVulnerabilityWindow(
  audit: PleaseAudit,
  sleepHours: number,
  energy: number
): VulnerabilityLevel {
  const score = audit.sleep + audit.food + audit.movement + audit.substances + audit.mastery;
  const sleepRisk = sleepHours < 6 ? "elevated" : "none";
  if (score <= 5 || sleepRisk === "elevated") return "high";
  if (score <= 8) return "moderate";
  return "low";
}

function generateNudge(window: VulnerabilityLevel): string | null {
  if (window === "high") return "Your body may be more vulnerable today. One small act of care can help.";
  if (window === "moderate") return "A few things are off. What's one small thing you can do right now?";
  return null;
}
```

**Novel**: The PLEASE audit *predicts* the risky window from the user's own sleep data and converts crisis-time coping into **baseline vulnerability reduction**—the other half of the DBT dialectic.

**Wire To**: `TodayScreen` nudge rail, `nilaContext.ts` (🟡), `proactiveSurfaceRouter` cooldown caps

**Cultural Adaptation (India)**:
- "Food" = "Did you eat a proper meal today?" (not "balanced nutrition")

- "Substances" = "Did you use anything to cope today?" (alcohol, substances)

## APPENDIX B: RESEARCH AGENT METHODOLOGY

Three parallel research agents were used to gather evidence:

### Agent 1: Foundational DBT Efficacy
- **Focus**: Landmark RCTs, meta-analyses, mechanism of change
- **Papers Verified**: Linehan 1991, 2006, 2015; McMain 2009; Kliem 2010; Panos 2014; DeCou 2019; Cristea 2017; Öst 2008; Stoffers-Winterling 2022; Neacsiu 2010; Stepp 2008; Probst 2018; Cavicchioli 2019; Durpoix 2023
- **Key Finding**: Skills use mediates outcomes; DBT reduces behavior, not ideation

### Agent 2: Skills Modules & Mechanisms
- **Focus**: DBT skills training, specific skills, chain analysis, treatment stages
- **Papers Verified**: Linehan 2015 (skills manual); Neacsiu 2014; Ritschel 2015; Valentine 2015
- **Key Finding**: Four modules (Mindfulness, Distress Tolerance, Emotion Regulation, Interpersonal Effectiveness); chain analysis is core idiographic procedure

### Agent 3: Digital/mHealth DBT + Bipolar/Crisis/Culture
- **Focus**: Digital DBT apps, mHealth, bipolar adaptation, crisis skills, cultural transfer
- **Papers Verified**: Rizvi 2011, 2016; Simon 2022; Astill Wright 2026; Widdershoven 2019; Van Dijk 2013; Goldstein 2024; Lynch 2003; Sevoz-Couche 2022; Sumińska 2026; Balban 2023; Bidargaddi 2018; Bryan 2017; Singh 2025; Abraham 2024; Ramaiya 2017
- **Key Finding**: In-the-moment coaching works but unaccompanied dumps are harmful; monitoring is inert; fixed 6 bpm pacer is equivalent to calibrated; cultural adaptation is feasible
